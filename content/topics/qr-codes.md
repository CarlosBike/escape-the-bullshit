title: QR Codes
navTitle: QR Codes
blurb: Look at what a code actually asks for.
order: 8
lead: >
  A QR code is just a picture of a web address. What matters is what the page behind it does with your data.
claim: >
  QR codes themselves are not dangerous — a code that opens a restaurant menu has no more data than typing the address. The concern is what happens on the other end. Scanning a code for a payment, a ticket or a loyalty card can silently hand over your identity, your location or your browsing history, and some codes have been used to redirect people to lookalike sites that harvest banking details. The practical habit is simply to read the domain before you sign in, to check the destination address after scanning, and to avoid scanning codes that have appeared unsolicited on a poster or in a message.
alternatives: >
  Type the address yourself when you know it. Read the domain before entering any personal or payment details. Keep your banking app's transaction alerts switched on. Use a QR scanner that shows the destination rather than opening it automatically.
responsible: >
  The businesses and apps you scan into are responsible for what they do with the data. The Code Conductors Committee in the UK is the body that took over responsibility for QR code standards and consumer safety from the British Standards Institution.
demand: >
  Clear scanning interfaces that show where a code will take you. Common standards for payment codes. Enforceability against lookalike phishing pages.
status: >
  Partly. There is now a UK-led international standard for QR codes, driven by the phone industry, which explicitly includes a common format for payments. Consumer education on QR phishing has improved, but the underlying phishing problem is very much live.
sources: >
  The Code Conductors Committee's QR code position paper and the UK NCSC guidance on QR code phishing.
advice: >
  No medical issue here — but do check the address before you log in to anything.
